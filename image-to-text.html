Extract Text from an Image with Free Online OCR

Retyping text from a screenshot, a photographed page or a forwarded image is slow and error-prone. Optical character recognition, or OCR, does the reading for you: it looks at the picture, recognises the letters and gives you plain text you can paste anywhere.

This guide explains how the AllToolsPro Image to Text tool works, what kind of pictures give good results, and where it falls short.

What OCR does

An image of text is just pixels to a computer. OCR analyses those pixels, finds lines and characters, and converts them into real text. The AllToolsPro tool uses the open-source Tesseract OCR engine, running in your browser through a JavaScript library. The result appears in a text box where you can edit it before copying.

You do not need to install an app or create an account on the page, which makes it a convenient option when you are working from a phone and only need the text from one picture.

Step-by-step: extract text

  1. Open the tool and tap Select Image to Scan. JPG, PNG and WebP images are listed as supported.
  2. Your picture appears as a preview and the scan begins automatically. A status line and progress bar show what the engine is doing, then Extracting Text with a percentage.
  3. When the scan finishes, the Extracted Text box fills with the result. Read through it and fix any mistakes directly in the box.
  4. Press Copy Text to copy everything to your clipboard, or New Scan to start with another image.

The first scan may take a little longer because the engine and its English language data have to load first, so you need an internet connection.

Do not leave the page while the progress bar is moving. Large or detailed images take longer, especially on older phones, because all the recognition work happens on your own device.

Where it is useful

  • Students: pull text out of a screenshot of a slide or a printed handout so you can quote or search it.
  • Office work: copy a table of figures or an address from a photographed letter instead of retyping.
  • Social media and chat: grab the text from a meme, notice or forwarded image.
  • Receipts and bills: copy reference numbers or amounts into your notes. Always double-check the digits.
  • Research: turn quotes from a photographed book page into notes.

Teachers and trainers use OCR to reuse text from old printed worksheets. Small businesses use it to copy details from printed visiting cards or product labels. Journalists and researchers use it to quote from screenshots without retyping long passages.

Tips for accurate results

  • Use clear, sharp pictures. Hold the phone steady and avoid blur.
  • Light evenly. Shadows across the page confuse the engine.
  • Straighten the image. If it is sideways or tilted, fix the orientation first with the Image Rotator.
  • Crop to the text. Removing borders and unrelated graphics reduces stray characters.
  • Prefer high contrast. Dark text on a light background works best.

Screenshots usually give better results than photos of paper, because the letters are perfectly sharp and evenly lit. If you must photograph a page, shoot straight on rather than at an angle, and fill the frame with the text so the letters are large in the picture.

Troubleshooting common problems

The text is full of strange characters

The image is probably too small, blurry or low in contrast. Try a better photo or a screenshot instead of a camera shot.

Hindi or other languages come out wrong

The tool is set to English only. Text in Hindi or other scripts will not be recognised correctly.

It shows an error and reloads

If recognition fails, the page asks you to try a clearer image. Also check that your connection is stable, since the engine files must load.

Handwriting is wrong

OCR works best on printed text. Handwriting is unreliable.

Proofreading the output

OCR is a time-saver, not a guarantee. Common slips include confusing the letter O with the digit 0, the letter l with the number 1, and merging or splitting words. Since the result sits in an editable box, scan it line by line against the original image. Pay extra attention to phone numbers, amounts, dates and names, where a single wrong character matters. Formatting such as bold text, columns and tables will not be preserved, so expect to tidy the layout in your own document afterwards.

Privacy and security

The page states that images are processed in the browser and are not sent to a server. In practice the OCR library and its English language data are downloaded from the internet when you scan, but your picture itself is not part of that download. Even so, think twice before scanning documents with Aadhaar, bank or password details, and always review the result yourself.

Remember too that the engine reads what it sees, not what you meant. If the original image has a typo or cut-off word, the result will repeat it.

Limits: when this isn&#39;t the right tool

The tool recognises English only, handles one image at a time, and gives plain text without preserving layout, columns or formatting. It cannot reliably read handwriting, heavily stylised fonts or very low-quality photos. For scanned multi-page PDFs or non-English documents, use software built for those jobs, and always proofread the output before relying on it.
