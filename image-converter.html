Convert Images to JPG, PNG or WebP: A Practical Guide

Sooner or later a website rejects your picture because it is in the wrong format. A portal wants JPG, a design file arrived as WebP, or a screenshot saved as PNG is far heavier than it needs to be. Converting the image takes seconds if you have the right tool.

This article walks through the AllToolsPro Image Converter, explains the three formats it offers, and shows how to pick the one that suits your purpose.

JPG, PNG and WebP in plain language

The converter supports three output formats, and each has a clear strength.

  • JPG: the safe, universal choice for photographs. It is accepted almost everywhere and compresses well.
  • PNG: lossless and sharp, so it suits screenshots, logos and graphics with text. Files are usually larger than a JPG of the same picture.
  • WebP: a modern format that typically gives smaller files at similar visual quality. It is popular for websites, though some older software and upload forms may not accept it.

If you are unsure which to pick, remember the simple rule: JPG for compatibility, WebP for smaller web files, PNG for sharp graphics. The tool lets you try all three on the same picture in a few seconds, so you never have to guess.

How to convert an image, step by step

  1. Open the tool and tap Select Image. The box notes that JPG, PNG and WebP are supported; other image types depend on what your browser can open.
  2. Your picture appears as a preview with two badges: Original size and an estimated new size.
  3. Under Target Format, choose JPG, WebP or PNG.
  4. Adjust the Compression Level slider (5% to 100%, default 80%). Lower values mean a smaller file.
  5. Check the estimate, then press Compress & Download. The file is saved with an AllToolsPro-Optimized name and the matching extension.

The estimate updates when you switch format or move the slider, which makes comparison easy. Try the same image as JPG, then WebP, then PNG and note the numbers; the right choice is often obvious in seconds.

When people convert images

  • Form uploads: a government or exam portal may accept only JPG. Confirm the current rules on the portal itself.
  • Website speed: bloggers and shop owners convert heavy PNGs to WebP or JPG to lighten pages.
  • Social media: a WebP image saved from the web may not post on every app, so converting to JPG helps.
  • Sharing screenshots: turning a large PNG screenshot into JPG makes it quicker to send over mobile data.

Content creators also use conversion for compatibility. A WebP picture downloaded from a website may refuse to open in an older photo app or editing program, while a JPG opens everywhere. Likewise, a designer sending a logo might prefer PNG so edges stay crisp. Students submitting scanned pages to an online portal may find that only one format is accepted, so a fast conversion saves a rejected submission.

Choosing settings that work

  • For a photo going to a form or email, choose JPG at 75-85%.
  • For a web page, try WebP and compare the estimate against JPG.
  • For a screenshot with small text, use PNG so letters stay crisp.
  • Look at the estimate after each change. Converting to a new format does not always make a file smaller; a photo-heavy picture saved as PNG can become much bigger.

A rule of thumb: pictures of real scenes (people, places, products) belong in JPG or WebP, while pictures made of flat colours, sharp lines and text belong in PNG. If you are not sure, convert to both and compare the estimate and the preview.

Mistakes to avoid

Expecting the slider to shrink a PNG

PNG is lossless, so the quality slider does not change a PNG file the way it changes JPG or WebP. If size matters, switch the format.

Transparency turning dark

JPG cannot store transparency. A logo with a see-through background may get a dark fill when saved as JPG. Use PNG or WebP if you need to keep it clear.

Converting again and again

Every pass through a lossy format like JPG loses a little detail. Convert once from the original rather than from a previous conversion.

Converting without losing quality

No lossy conversion is perfectly free of loss, but you can limit the damage. Keep the compression level high, around 90% or more, when image quality is the priority, such as for a portfolio picture. Drop it to 70-80% only when size matters more. Always convert from your best original, not from a screenshot of a screenshot, and open the downloaded file once to confirm it looks right before you delete the source.

Privacy

The page reads your file in the browser and redraws it on a hidden canvas to produce the new format. Its code has no upload step, so your picture stays on your device during conversion. As with any online tool, avoid handling documents you consider extremely sensitive, and read the privacy policy if you are unsure.

One more point: the output file name starts with AllToolsPro-Optimized, so rename it to something meaningful before you attach it to an application or send it to a client.

Limits of this converter

It handles one image at a time and offers just three target formats. It does not support GIF, BMP, TIFF or HEIC output, there is no batch mode, and the image keeps its original dimensions. If you also need to change pixel size, use the Image Resizer afterwards. Whether a particular input type opens depends on your browser.
