Keyword Density Checker: Spot Repetition Before You Publish

Every writer repeats words without noticing. Over a 1,200-word article, "best", "online" or the name of your product can sneak in dozens of times, and the text starts to sound mechanical. A keyword density checker puts a number on that repetition so you can see it at a glance.

Used well, it is a proofreading aid. Used badly, it turns into a hunt for a magic percentage, which does not exist. This article shows how the tool works and how to apply it sensibly.

What keyword density means

Keyword density is the number of times a word appears divided by the total number of words, shown as a percentage. If a word appears 10 times in a 500-word text, its density is 2%.

The Keyword Density Checker calculates this for the most frequent words in whatever you paste. It also shows the total word count, the character count and the number of unique words, so you get a quick profile of the text.

How to use the checker, step by step

  1. Paste your article, product description or page copy into the large text box.
  2. Click Analyze Keywords.
  3. Look at the three summary cards: Total Words, Characters and Unique Keywords.
  4. Read the Keyword Density Analysis table, which lists the top 15 words with their Count and Density (%).
  5. Edit your text, paste it again and re-run the analysis. Use Clear All to start over.

How the analysis is calculated

Knowing the rules helps you read the results correctly. The tool converts the text to lowercase, removes punctuation and splits it into words. It ignores single-letter words and a short list of common words such as "the", "and", "of" and "with". Each remaining word is counted, and the table shows the 15 most frequent ones.

Two consequences are worth remembering. First, it counts single words only, so a phrase like "free pdf compressor" appears as separate words. Second, the tool is built for English-style text, and words in other scripts, including Hindi in Devanagari, may not be counted properly. Percentages are based on all words, including the ignored common ones.

Use the numbers as a writing check, not a target

Search engines do not publish an ideal percentage, and no fixed number guarantees a ranking. Pages rank because they answer a search clearly, cover the topic well and earn trust. Chasing a density figure often produces awkward writing.

Instead, use the table to answer practical questions:

  • Does your main topic word appear naturally, in the title, an early paragraph and a few headings?
  • Is any non-essential word unexpectedly near the top?
  • Does one word dominate so much that the text feels repetitive when read aloud?

If the answer to the last question is yes, vary your wording with synonyms and related terms, or restructure the sentence.

Good ways to use it in real work

  • Blog posts: check that the page really focuses on the topic you intended.
  • Product pages: catch descriptions where the brand name or a feature word is repeated in every sentence.
  • Freelance writing: review a client draft for repetition before delivery.
  • Competitor-style comparison: paste your own old and new versions to see how the emphasis shifted.

For an Indian business site writing in English for local customers, this also helps you notice repeated city names or service words that were added to "cover" many searches but now read like a list.

Common mistakes and troubleshooting

Pasting a whole web page including menus. Navigation text and footers inflate the counts. Paste only the main content.

Expecting phrases. The table shows single words. Use your own search in the text to count a multi-word phrase.

Odd words in the list. Numbers, brand names and common verbs can appear. They are counted like any other word.

Nothing happens. If the box is empty the tool asks you to paste text. Very short text gives unstable percentages, so analyse at least a few hundred words.

Privacy and limits

The analysis runs in your browser, and the text is processed on your device. It is still wise not to paste confidential client material anywhere you do not need to.

The tool does not analyse headings, links, search intent or competitors, does not understand synonyms, and does not check grammar. It is a frequency counter. Pair it with a careful human read, and think about what the reader needs, not what a percentage says.
