How to Create Strong Passwords (and Keep Them Safe)

Most account break-ins do not involve clever hacking. They happen because a password was short, easy to guess, or reused from another site that later leaked. Humans are poor at making random passwords. We reach for names, birth years and keyboard patterns, and attackers know it.

A password generator takes that choice away from you. This guide shows how to use the tool, what its settings really do, and, more importantly, the habits that decide whether a good password actually protects you.

What the generator does

The tool builds a password by picking characters at random from the sets you enable. You can choose a length from 6 to 32 and include uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers and symbols. A strength meter beside the result gives a quick rating of Weak, Medium or Very Strong.

The generation happens in your browser. The page does not send the password to our servers, and it is not saved by the page.

How to generate a password, step by step

  1. Open the page; a password is already generated for you.
  2. Drag the Password Length slider. The number beside it updates, and a new password appears each time.
  3. Tick or untick Include Uppercase (A-Z), Include Lowercase (a-z), Include Numbers (0-9) and Include Symbols (!@#$).
  4. Press Generate New Password for another one.
  5. Click the copy button next to the result and paste it straight into your password manager or the sign-up form.

If you untick every option, the box shows a prompt to select at least one.

Length and uniqueness beat clever tricks

Two things matter most. First, length: every extra character multiplies the work an attacker needs. The tool suggests 12 or more, and for important accounts such as email, banking and UPI-linked apps, 16 or more is a better habit. Second, uniqueness: use a different password for every account. If one site is breached, a reused password lets attackers try it everywhere else.

Substituting symbols for letters in a word you chose yourself, like turning "Password" into "P@ssw0rd", adds almost nothing, because cracking tools try those patterns first. Random generation avoids that.

Use a password manager, and turn on 2FA

Nobody can remember dozens of random 16-character passwords, and you should not try. Store them in a reputable password manager, protected by one strong master passphrase, such as four or five unrelated words that you can remember.

Then switch on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered, especially for email, which can reset all your other accounts. Prefer an authenticator app or a hardware key over SMS codes when possible. Together, a unique password plus a second factor stops most everyday attacks.

Everyday scenarios

  • New social or shopping account: generate 16+ characters with all four sets and save it in your manager before submitting the form.
  • Wi-Fi router: a long random password with letters and numbers is easier to type on a TV or console than one full of symbols.
  • Shared family accounts: create a unique one, share it through your manager's sharing feature, not WhatsApp or a sticky note.
  • Sites that reject symbols: untick Include Symbols and increase the length to compensate.

Common mistakes and troubleshooting

Generating a password but not saving it. The moment you leave the page, it is gone. Copy it first.

Reusing a "strong" password. Strength does not help if you use it in ten places.

Trusting the meter blindly. The strength indicator is a simple rule-of-thumb based on length and character types. It cannot know whether a password has leaked elsewhere. Treat it as a rough hint.

Characters rejected by a site. Some sites block certain symbols. Regenerate without symbols and use more length.

Not every set is guaranteed. Because characters are picked randomly, a short password may occasionally miss one of the selected types. Generate again if a site demands all four.

Privacy, limits and honest caveats

The tool uses the browser's standard random function to choose characters. That is fine for everyday accounts, but if you are protecting something extremely sensitive, such as a company's admin credentials or a crypto wallet, use the generator built into a trusted password manager or security tool that relies on cryptographic randomness.

The tool does not store, check for breaches or remember your passwords, and it cannot make a site store them safely. It also lets you choose as few as 6 characters; avoid that range for anything that matters. When a site offers passkeys, they are worth using too.

How long should my password be?

Use at least 12 characters, and 16 or more for important accounts like email and banking. Longer is stronger.

Is it safe to use a generated password from a website?

The password is generated in your browser and not sent to our servers. For the most sensitive accounts, a generator built into a trusted password manager is a better choice.

Should I also enable two-factor authentication?
